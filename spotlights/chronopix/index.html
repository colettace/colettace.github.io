From microscopy video to published insight

ChronoPix: bringing Python, signal processing, image analysis, and machine learning together to study cardiac calcium signaling.

Baltimore, Maryland

At NIH, I helped biological collaborators turn complex experimental data into analysis and figures they could interpret. ChronoPix exemplifies that work: a Python research pipeline for calcium signaling videos of mouse sinoatrial node tissue—the tissue involved in the heart’s pacemaker.

My analysis and visualization were incorporated into the 2022 paper The heart’s pacemaker mimics brain cytoarchitecture and function: Novel interstitial cells expose complexity of the SAN, which I coauthored.

The challenge

A microscopy video contains more than a sequence of images. Each pixel also records a signal over time. Understanding how calcium activity varies across tissue requires connecting that time-dependent behavior with its spatial location.

I brought mathematics, signal processing, image analysis, modeling, and machine learning together to help collaborators explore those patterns.

How ChronoPix makes timing visible

The pipeline treats video as a three-dimensional array: image height, image width, and time. Each pixel contributes a calcium fluorescence time series.

  1. Analyze the signal. Apply a real-input fast Fourier transform along the time axis at each pixel to obtain amplitude and phase at each frequency bin.
  2. Find prominent frequencies. Average amplitudes across the image to identify frequencies of interest in the preparation.
  3. Map a selected frequency. Extract spatial maps of amplitude and phase for that frequency.
  4. Make the pattern visible. Encode phase as hue on a cyclic color wheel and amplitude as brightness, showing relative timing while making stronger signals more prominent.

I also explored unsupervised clustering of Fourier amplitude signatures to identify functionally distinct regions. In the selected-frequency visualization, color represents phase, while brightness represents amplitude.

A practical scientific use: seeing where signaling changes

ChronoPix gave us a way to compare experimental conditions spatially, showing how signal timing and coordination changed within the tissue. In the control and S100B comparison pictured above, the maps made changes in local calcium signaling visible. The paper reports that S100B application desynchronized calcium signaling in HCN4-expressing cells.

By characterizing both control and S100B conditions, we could see changes in behavior and identify specific tissue regions whose signaling became dysregulated—connecting the experimental intervention to a spatial pattern that helped inform the finding.

The practical value was being able to ask both how signaling changed and where in the tissue it changed.

The outcome

The workflow turned high-dimensional microscopy video into interpretable spatial evidence and publication figures. The 2022 paper applies Chronopix maps in Figure 14 to compare calcium activity under control and S100B conditions.

For me, the achievement was connecting hands-on pipeline development with mathematical analysis and scientific communication: helping collaborators move from a difficult dataset to a result they could examine, discuss, and publish.
